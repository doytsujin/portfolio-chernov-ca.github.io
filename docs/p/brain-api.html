Brain API: decide and record what AI agents may do

2026 – present · Independent work, unaffiliated with any employer.

An intent becomes a plan only through a decision that applies policy, chooses among alternatives and keeps the reason as a record.

An intent-aware control plane for policy-governed agentic systems. A goal enters as a first-class control object: candidates are generated, policy removes the inadmissible ones, the rest are ranked and one is selected, and the choice is kept as a decision artifact, a durable, versioned record of which policies applied, which alternatives were rejected, and why.

Control planes, workflow engines and service meshes govern resources: services, deployments, network flows, execution graphs. Agentic and tool-augmented systems have moved application logic toward intent-driven, adaptive execution, and those abstractions cannot represent a goal as a first-class control object, cannot enforce policy over the mapping from intent to execution plan, and cannot record why one execution path was chosen over its alternatives. The control logic ends up in application code, where it is brittle, opaque and hard to govern.

Brain API puts a decision layer between intent and execution. An intent, such as a compliance officer asking for a regulatory report on a dataset, yields candidate capabilities; policies remove the inadmissible ones, context signals inform the rest, one is selected, and a plan for it is handed to an execution substrate through an adapter. The decision is kept beside the plan as a decision artifact with seven parts: the intent reference, the policy versions, a context snapshot, the candidate set, the selected plan, the rejected alternatives with their cause, and the rationale.

Admission control and Brain API both act before execution, but admission control adjudicates one proposed object, while Brain API resolves one goal among alternatives and keeps the choice. The demo shows the difference from an auditor’s side: on cases from the OPA Gatekeeper constraint library, six of six audit questions are answered from a decision artifact, against two of six from an admission log.

A prototype of the decision layer was evaluated against two external policy corpora the author did not write. On the OPA Gatekeeper constraint library it agrees with the library’s published verdicts on 42 of 42 encodable cases, 19 admit and 23 deny, and in selection 20 of 20 trials chose the alternative the corpus labels compliant, in one pass. On Cedar example policies, labeled by differential testing against Cedar’s reference implementation, it agrees on 78 of 81, all 71 denials and 7 of 10 allows; that deliberately dissimilar domain exposed three defects in the model, among them a default-allow assumption that would have inverted every authorization policy. The evaluation covers policy filtering and selection; candidate generation, context signals, ranking and plan synthesis are not measured, nor is decision latency under load.

Two projects here build on it and are described on their own pages: System One, a small model trained to judge which admitted capability a request is for, and Agentium DB, the first use case measured with Brain API itself.

Transferable principle: govern the choice, not only the object, and keep the alternatives a decision rejected, with their cause, beside the plan it produced.
